## Q1. What is the cross-country correlation between education spending (total, per student, by level) and innovation outputs (R&D intensity, researchers per million, patents per capita, TFP growth), raw and conditional on income? Is it causal?

### Takeaway
Public education spending as % of GDP is almost unrelated to income (r = 0.05, n = 185). Its correlation with innovation outputs is modest: r ≈ 0.32–0.40 with log researchers per million and log R&D intensity, and partial r ≈ 0.20–0.36 after controlling for income. It has no relation to TFP growth. Human-capital stocks are far stronger correlates: the HCI correlates r = 0.90 with log researchers (partial 0.63). Controlling for HCI cuts the income elasticity of researchers per million from 1.35 to 0.37. Macro correlations are not causal. The credible causal evidence is micro or regional (new universities and colleges → +20% to +62% patents locally) and modest at the macro level (Aghion et al.: +0.04 to +0.07 pp growth per $1,000 per cohort member).

#### Published evidence on the spending–innovation link and causality
- **A021 | Aghion, Boustan, Hoxby & Vandenbussche (2009), "The Causal Impact of Education on Economic Growth: Evidence from the United States".** Src: [Brookings BPEA draft PDF](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2009_spring_bpea_aghion_etal.pdf). Cov: US state panel from the 1960s onward (the paper refers to states' 1963 frontier positions). Meas: growth effects of exogenous education-spending shocks by type. Key:
  - At the frontier, $1,000 of research-university spending per person in the cohort raises growth by **0.04 pp**. A typical shock (~$500 lasting ~6 years) raises growth by about **0.12 pp** at its peak.
  - At the frontier, $1,000 of four-year college spending raises growth by **0.07 pp**. The typical shock is only ~$150, so its effect is ~0.011 pp.
  - Far from the frontier, $1,000 of research spending *lowers* growth by **0.07 pp** (through migration or crowd-out), while $1,000 of four-year college spending raises it by **0.03 pp**.
  - Two-year college spending is insignificant everywhere.
  - Without the frontier interaction, the research coefficient is +0.02 pp.

  Yr: 2009 (BPEA draft). Q: the instruments are political committee memberships (federal appropriations for research; state-legislature committee chairs for 4- and 2-year colleges), with a 2-year lag from appointment to spending. Skilled migration complicates the state-level interpretation. Use: the best macro-causal magnitude. Per-capita spending effects are small (hundredths of a pp per $1,000), and the type of spending matters by frontier distance.
- **A022 | Toivanen & Väänänen (2016), "Education and Invention", REStat 98(2): 382–396.** Src: [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/education-and-invention/); [CEPR DP8537](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/8537.html). Cov: Finland, Finnish inventors on USPTO patents. Meas: causal effect of an MSc in engineering on patenting. Key: the IV estimate is positive and larger than OLS (OLS is biased downward). In the counterfactual, the three newly founded technical universities raised USPTO patents by Finnish inventors by about **20%**. Yr: 2016. Q: the IV is distance to the nearest technical university; replication data is on Harvard Dataverse (doi 10.7910/DVN/XSYPO2). Use: a causal elasticity from tertiary STEM supply to invention, about +20% national patents from a few new technical universities.

### Inferences
- In the raw cross-section, education *spending effort* (% GDP) is a weak and largely income-independent signal. Human-capital *stocks* (HCI, HLO, tertiary attainment) carry most of the information about a country's innovation inputs. The game should map budget → stocks (schooling, learning, tertiary graduates) → S, not budget → S directly.
- Conditional slopes from A011 and A013 imply about +16–25% researchers or R&D per extra pp of GDP spent on education, holding income and test scores fixed. Given A018 and A019 (no within-country or lagged effect), treat these as upper bounds dominated by selection.
- Causal evidence (A021–A027) shows positive but modest effects. A natural calibration is that a sustained tertiary/STEM supply shock causing +10% graduates yields roughly +5–20% patents over 1–3 decades (Toivanen–Väänänen's 3 universities → +20%). Macro growth effects of education money should be of order 0.01–0.1 pp per year.
- TFP growth is raw-uncorrelated with education but conditionally positively correlated. This fits a catch-up mechanism, which the game currently lacks (no spillovers between nations). If the game adds technology diffusion, human capital should scale the absorption rate.

- No cross-country *causal* estimate of education spending on R&D/patents exists to my knowledge. Macro identification relies on US states (A021) or regions (A024).
- Carneiro–Liu–Salvanes magnitudes and Kantor–Whalley/Hausman local university spillover magnitudes were not extracted.

## Q2. Does the composition of education matter near vs far from the technology frontier (tertiary/STEM vs basic)?

### Takeaway
The theory and the OECD/US-state evidence say yes. Tertiary (and top-tail) skills matter more for innovation near the frontier, while basic and secondary schooling serves imitation and adoption far from it (Vandenbussche–Aghion–Meghir 2006; Aghion et al. 2009; Ang–Madsen–Islam 2011; Madsen 2014). Historical evidence points the same way: upper-tail knowledge, not literacy, predicted industrial growth (Squicciarini–Voigtländer 2015). Micro evidence shows STEM degrees change the *direction* and amount of invention. My own world cross-section for 2000–19 does not reproduce the interaction, so the effect is not a robust raw stylized fact.

## Q3. Quality vs quantity: knowledge capital (test scores) and growth, learning-adjusted schooling, and innovation

### Takeaway
Cognitive skills dominate years of schooling in cross-country growth and innovation regressions. In Hanushek–Woessmann, 1 SD in test scores ≈ +2 pp annual growth over 40 years. In my data, HLO and HCI correlate 0.83–0.90 with log researchers and remain significant with income controlled, while schooling years lose significance. Critiques (Komatsu–Rappleye: with lagged growth the relation largely disappears; reverse-causality and measurement concerns from Bils–Klenow and Pritchett) mean the 2 pp figure should be treated as an upper bound. Development-accounting estimates put human capital at 1/5–1/2 (Angrist et al. 2021) to ~60% (Hendricks–Schoellman 2018) of income differences.

- I did not find a 2023–2026 peer-reviewed re-estimation of the Hanushek–Woessmann regression using HLO data with proper lags. This is an open gap.
- The cross-country link from *test scores to patents or R&D* specifically (rather than to GDP growth) is rarely estimated in published work; my own A012–A013 are descriptive.
- No HCI vintage after 2020 was found in WDI as of the 2026-10-08 update.

## Q4. Who becomes an inventor? "Lost Einsteins", exposure effects, family income, and Nordic evidence

### Takeaway
The probability of becoming an inventor is extremely unequal. US children from top-1% families are 10× as likely as below-median children to invent, and test scores explain less than one-third of the gap. Childhood exposure to inventors causally raises invention, specifically by technology class and gender. If women, minorities and low-income children invented at the rate of high-income white men, there would be four times as many inventors (Bell et al. 2019). In Finland, IQ is the dominant predictor, but parental income and education still matter; a father with a PhD adds +3 pp on a 0.9% base. In Denmark, PhDs are 20× more likely to invent. Better allocation of talent explains 20–40% of US per-person output growth 1960–2010.

Cov: 1.2 million US inventors (patent records linked to tax and school records); intergenerational sample born 1980–84; NYC school-district sample. Key: children from **top-1%** families are **10×** as likely to become inventors as those from below-median families. The gaps persist among children with similar early math scores. Yr: 2017 WP / 2019. Q: descriptive gradients plus a quasi-experimental movers design (the age at which a family moves). Use: an "inventor propensity" function that depends on family income and exposure, not just ability.

### Inferences
- The game's S = population × f(income) misses a large allocation margin. With exposure and access, the same population could supply up to about 4× more inventors (Bell), and talent allocation can account for 20–40% of growth over 50 years (HHJK). A natural extension is S = pop × p(top-tail skill share) × access(inequality, exposure).
- Exposure effects (A065) provide a *within-nation* spillover: the current inventor share raises future inventor supply. This fits a model with no cross-nation spillovers and light-speed delays.
- The education level matters multiplicatively for inventor rates (PhD 20×; STEM-PhD parents >6×), so tertiary and doctoral capacity is a key lever.

### Gaps
- No Sweden-specific inventor-origins paper with numbers was retrieved. Denmark (A035/A067) and Finland (A066) cover the Nordic analogue.
- The magnitude of Bell et al.'s commuting-zone exposure elasticity (per SD of local inventor density) was not extracted. Only the father's-industry exposure (+25.3% per SD) was confirmed.
- No cross-country dataset of inventor rates by parental background exists. All evidence is single-country.

## Q5. Inventor and skilled migration and innovation (brain drain / brain gain)

### Takeaway
Inventors, especially superstars, are mobile in response to taxes. The location elasticity with respect to the net-of-tax rate is about 1 for foreign superstars and about 0.03 for domestic ones (Akcigit–Baslandze–Stantcheva 2016). Immigrants are 16% of US inventors but produced 23% of patents and, through spillovers, account for about 32–36% of aggregate US innovation. Historical shocks show large field-level effects (German-Jewish émigrés: +31% US patents in their fields). Lottery evidence on marginal H-1B workers shows little effect, so the gains come from top talent. Emigration can also induce "brain gain" in origin countries (each Filipino nurse migrant → 9 extra licensed nurses), and migrants' gains spill back to origin collaborators (+16%/yr).

Cov: USPTO and EPO inventor panels since 1977, 8 countries. Key: superstar (top-1%) inventors' location choices respond to top tax rates. The elasticity of the number of **domestic** superstar inventors with respect to the net-of-tax rate is about **0.03**; for **foreign** superstar inventors it is about **1**. Inventors in multinationals respond more; inventors whose company does much of its research in a country respond less. Yr: 2015 WP / 2016. ### Inferences
- For the game, migration of researchers between nations or colonies should be very elastic for top talent (ε ≈ 1 with respect to net returns) and inelastic for natives (≈ 0.03). Migrants should be about 1.4× as productive (23% of patents from 16% of inventors) and should raise co-located researchers' productivity.
- Light-speed constraints matter: Prato's +16%/yr origin-collaborator spillover depends on continued collaboration. Over interstellar distances that channel would largely vanish, so brain drain would be more harmful than on Earth. This is an inference, not evidence.
- Marginal versus top migrants: the clean lottery null (A076) against the large historical and superstar effects (A072–A074, A077) suggests modelling migrant impact as concentrated in the top tail.

## Q6. Time lags: how long from education spending changes to measurable innovation and productivity effects?

### Takeaway
The lags are long. Per-pupil spending affects test scores within about 4 years and attainment and wages 12–25 years later. Capital spending takes about 5 years to show effects. Inventors' mean age is 44, and age at first invention is rising 0.6 years per decade, so a schooling cohort's innovation peak arrives 20–40 years after it enters school. A structural Danish model needs about 30 years for education subsidies to go halfway to their steady-state effect, and education-slot expansion overtakes R&D subsidies only after about 6 years. My own within-country panel finds no detectable effect of spending on researchers, R&D or patents at 0–15 year lags. Effects take longer than about 15 years to show and are buried in noise.

### Cited Findings
- **A086 | Akcigit, Pearce & Prato (2025), policy transition dynamics.** Src: [NBER w27862](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27862). Key: all simulated policies take **more than 10 years** to get halfway to the new steady state. **Education subsidies take 30 years** to reach the halfway point. R&D subsidies act immediately (lab equipment), whereas expanding educational slots "takes some time and surpasses R&D after **six years**". Education subsidies eventually become the most effective tool through researcher quality, as talented new entrants become team leaders. The authors warn that evaluations based on short time spans understate education effects. Use: half-life parameters. Education → effective S has a half-life of about 30 years; R&D subsidy → S is near-immediate.
- **A087 | Jackson & Mackevicius (2024) lag structure (see A026).** Src: [NBER w28517](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28517). Key: test-score and college-going effects arise from 4 years of exposure. Capital-spending effects take **about five years** to materialize. Use: budget → HLO lag of about 4–5 years.
- **A088 | Jackson, Johnson & Persico (2016), lag (see A027).** Src: [NBER w20847](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20847). Key: outcomes measured in adulthood (born 1955–85, followed to 2011) respond to 12 years of K–12 spending. The causal chain from school spending to wages spans 15–30 years. Use: budget → adult human capital lag.
- **A089 | Bell et al. (2019), age profile of inventors.** Src: [NBER w24062](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24062). Key: the mean inventor age is **44**. Childhood exposure (before about age 20) affects patenting decades later. Use: the lag from childhood education to inventive output is 25–35 years.
- **A090 | Jones (2009), rising training time (see A071).** Src: [NBER w11360](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11360). - **A095 | Jones (2002), "Sources of U.S. Economic Growth in a World of Ideas", AER 92(1) (classic; from published abstract, not re-fetched this session).** Src: [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282802760015757). Education-driven growth is transitional once attainment saturates.

### Inferences
- Suggested game lag structure: a budget change → learning (HLO) within 4–5 years → schooling and tertiary flows → the adult human-capital stock through cohort replacement over about 40 years → S quality. Half-life of education → effective S of about 30 years (Akcigit–Pearce–Prato). R&D subsidies act within 1–2 years.
- Since information travels at light speed, inter-colony diffusion would add lags on top of these domestic ones. Even with zero distance, education effects on innovation are generational.
- Because growth from education is transitional (Jones 2002), in the very long run (2300–2800) human capital should determine *levels* of S and A, while β ≈ 3 governs the slowdown.

### Gaps
- No study directly estimates the distributed lag from *national* education spending to patents. Evidence is assembled from micro links (spending → attainment, attainment → invention, age profiles).
- My panel covers only 30 years (1995–2024). Lags beyond 15 years cannot be tested with WDI R&D and researcher data.

## Q8. How should these findings map onto the game's mechanisms (Jones idea production function with β ≈ 3, S = pop × f(income), human capital raising worker productivity, an education budget)?

### Takeaway
The cleanest evidence-based change is to make the researcher pool depend on human capital, not just income. Controlling for HCI cuts the income elasticity of researchers per million from 1.35 to 0.37 (own computation, n = 123). Researchers should also be quality-weighted by tertiary/PhD attainment and the top tail of test scores. Education spending should act on S only with generational lags (half-life ≈ 30 years). Migration of top inventors should be elastic, and childhood exposure to inventors offers a within-nation feedback.

### Cited Findings
- Researchers scale with income at elasticity 1.343 (R² = 0.685, n = 127). Once HCI is included, the income elasticity falls to 0.365 and R² rises to 0.815 (HCI coefficient 8.908 log points per HCI unit) — own computation from [WDI SP.POP.SCIE.RD.P6](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/all/indicator/SP.POP.SCIE.RD.P6?format=json&per_page=20000&date=1990:2025) and [HD.HCI.OVRL](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/all/indicator/HD.HCI.OVRL?format=json&per_page=20000&date=2010:2025).
- Reduced form: ln(res/M) = 0.164·edu%GDP + 1.285·HLO/100 + 0.720·ln y (R² = 0.812, n = 117) — own computation (A013), same sources.
- PhDs are 20× more likely to invent; education subsidies take 30 years to reach half their effect; slot expansion overtakes R&D subsidies after 6 years — [Akcigit, Pearce & Prato (NBER w27862)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27862).
- The share of top performers matters about 4× more per pp than the basic-literacy share (1.3 vs 0.3 pp growth per 10 pp) — [Hanushek & Woessmann (NBER w14633)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14633).
- Inventor supply could be about 4× higher with equal access, and exposure raises invention rates (+25.3% per SD of father's-industry inventor share) — [Bell et al. (NBER w24062)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24062).
- Top-inventor location elasticity is about 1 for foreigners and 0.03 for natives — [Akcigit, Baslandze & Stantcheva (NBER w21024)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21024). Immigrants are 16% of inventors and produce 23% of patents — [Bernstein et al. (NBER w30797)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30797).
- Education-spending effects on growth are small at the macro level (0.04–0.07 pp per $1,000 per cohort member at the frontier) — [Aghion et al. 2009](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2009_spring_bpea_aghion_etal.pdf).
- Education-driven growth is largely transitional (about 80% of 1950–93 US growth from rising attainment and research intensity) — [Jones 2002](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282802760015757).
- The tertiary-share × frontier interaction is not visible in the 2000–19 world cross-section (interaction −0.375, se 1.840) — own computation (A020) from [PWT 11.0](https://doi.org/10.34894/FABVLR) and [Barro–Lee v3](https://barrolee.github.io/BarroLeeDataSet/BLData/BL_v3_MF2564.csv).

### Inferences
- **Suggested target set (raw-data stylized facts with exact numbers):**
  - (T1) researchers per million vs ln GDPpc elasticity = 1.34 (A007);
  - (T2) R&D/GDP elasticity = 0.66 (A006);
  - (T3) patents per million elasticity = 1.63 (A008);
  - (T4) patents per researcher ≈ 0.022/yr with weak income dependence (A010);
  - (T5) public education spending ≈ 4–5% of GDP and flat in income (A001);
  - (T6) HCI rises 0.109 per ln-income unit (A050);
  - (T7) partial correlation of HCI with ln researchers = 0.63 (A012);
  - (T8) TFP growth uncorrelated with education raw but positively conditional on income (A015);
  - (T9) no short-run (≤15-year) within-country response of innovation to education spending (A018).
- **Mechanism suggestions:**
  - (M1) S = pop × f(y, HLO, tertiary share), using the A013 coefficients.
  - (M2) A quality weight on S that rises with the PhD/tertiary share and the top-tail test-score share.
  - (M3) Education budget → HLO (4–5 yr lag; Jackson–Mackevicius: +0.035 SD per $1,000/pupil for 4 years) and → tertiary entry (+2.65 pp college-going per $1,000/pupil), with effects on S through cohort turnover (half-life ~30 yr).
  - (M4) Optional: an exposure feedback from current inventor share to future inventor propensity (Bell).
  - (M5) Optional: inter-nation migration of top researchers with elasticity ≈ 1, a 1.4× productivity premium and team spillovers (Bernstein; Prato).
  - (M6) If a frontier or diffusion module is added later, human capital should scale the catch-up speed (Nelson–Phelps; A015), with the tertiary threshold around 0.21 (VAM).
- Correlation versus causation for game design: T1–T9 are raw or conditional correlations and suit "targets". The magnitudes inside mechanisms M1–M5 should come from the causal estimates (A021–A027, A062–A065, A072–A080), which are generally smaller than the raw correlations.

### Gaps
- No single study provides a full causal chain from national education spending to national TFP growth. The game's mechanism necessarily chains separately identified links.
- Cross-country data on STEM graduate shares, PhD output and inventor counts by education were not compiled in this session.
