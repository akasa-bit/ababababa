## 1. What exactly did the 2025 Nobel committee award, which mechanisms does it describe, and which empirical regularities does it stress?

### Takeaway
The 2025 prize was "for having explained innovation-driven economic growth". Half went to Joel Mokyr "for having identified the prerequisites for sustained growth through technological progress". The other half went jointly to Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt "for the theory of sustained growth through creative destruction".
- **Mokyr:** sustained growth needs three things together:
  - a positive feedback loop between "propositional" knowledge (science) and "prescriptive" knowledge (technique), which took hold with the Industrial Enlightenment;
  - upper-tail mechanical competence;
  - a society open to change, with institutions that let the winners of new technology overcome vested interests.
- **Aghion–Howitt (1992):** growth comes from quality-ladder innovations that steal business from incumbents.
  - The growth rate equals the creative-destruction rate times the step size.
  - The decentralized growth rate can be too high (business stealing) or too low (intertemporal spillovers and monopoly pricing).
  - The step-by-step extension gives the escape-competition and discouragement effects and the inverted-U.
- **Regularities the committee stresses:** in a typical year more than 10% of US firms enter or exit; churn happens mostly within industries; industries with more exit and job destruction have faster labor-productivity growth; about one quarter of TFP growth comes from entry/exit; dynamism has declined since the mid-2000s, alongside the productivity slowdown, rising markups and concentration.

### Inferences
- The prize lets a model treat innovation as having three layers:
  - **Knowledge-base layer (Mokyr):** the stock of science and its access cost set R&D productivity.
  - **Firm-level layer (Aghion–Howitt):** quality ladders, business stealing, entry and exit.
  - **Political-economy layer (both):** incumbents lose value as the creative-destruction rate rises, so they lobby to block entry. A society's openness decides whether innovations diffuse.
- A Jones-type idea production function (what the game has now) captures only the aggregate knowledge-accumulation part. It lacks the distributive conflict and policy margins the committee emphasizes.
- The committee's two quantitative regularities are the easiest to hit:
  - more than 10% of firms enter or exit per year;
  - about 25% of TFP growth from entry/exit, and roughly half including reallocation.
- The committee's take on AI is optimistic but conditional ("in the right regulatory and societal environment"). That reads as a multiplier on the science–technique feedback loop, not a guaranteed boost.

## 2. Creative destruction and firm dynamics: how much growth comes from entrants, incumbents' own innovation and creative destruction? How large are missing growth, the decline in dynamism and the frontier–laggard divergence?

### Takeaway
In US Census microdata, growth comes mostly from incumbents improving their own products:
- Garcia-Macia, Hsieh & Klenow (GHK): own innovation 59–73%, creative destruction 23–28%, new varieties 5–13%; entrants (firms under 5 years old) 21–33%.
- Klenow & Li: own innovation 60%, new varieties 27%, creative destruction 13%. Yet new and young firms (ages 0–5) produce almost half of growth, three times their employment share.
- Creative destruction biases measured growth down by about 0.5 pp/yr (published; 0.64 pp/yr in the WP).
- US business dynamism fell sharply. My BDS calculations: the firm startup rate fell from 13.7% (1978) to roughly 8–9% (2010s); job reallocation fell from about 30% (1978–89 average) to about 23% (2008–19); the employment share of firms aged 0–5 fell from 17–18% (mid-1980s) to about 10.6% (2019–23).
- The global productivity frontier (top 5% of firms) pulled away from the rest in the 2000s: roughly 2%/yr frontier MFP growth in manufacturing vs under 0.1%/yr for laggards. The leading explanation is weaker knowledge diffusion from leaders to laggards.

### Inferences
- **Targets for a creative-destruction layer on top of the existing Jones A-update:**
  - Split each nation-sector's A growth into own improvement (about 60–70%), creative destruction (about 25%, two-thirds by entrants), and varieties (about 5–15%).
  - The entrant share of growth is about 20–33% (GHK) and up to about 50% for firms aged 0–5 (Klenow–Li).
  - Implied 5-year hazards from GHK: an incumbent's own improvement about 69%; displacement about 31% (entrant 21.5%, other incumbent 9.4%); quality step about 7.5% per jump. That is roughly 6%/yr displacement per product line. Treat this as an approximation: the 5-year probabilities convert nonlinearly.
- **Dynamism targets:** firm entry about 8–12%/yr, exit about 8–10%/yr, job reallocation about 22–30%/yr, young-firm (0–5) employment share about 11–18%. A "mature" or stagnant economy should drift toward the lower ends, with growth falling about 0.5–1 pp.
- **A natural single driver for a late-game slowdown** is lower knowledge diffusion from leader to laggard (Akcigit–Ates; Andrews et al.). It produces concentration, a falling entry rate, frontier–laggard divergence (about 2 pp/yr frontier vs about 0%/yr laggards), and a slowdown of about 0.6 pp/yr together.
- **Measurement:** a game showing "official statistics" could understate true growth by about 0.5 pp/yr when creative destruction is high (missing growth).

## 3. Competition and innovation: the inverted-U and later tests, escape-competition vs discouragement, market size, directed technical change and green innovation

### Takeaway
- **Competition:** the evidence supports a non-monotonic, gap-dependent effect.
  - Competition raises innovation for firms neck-and-neck with rivals or near the frontier (escape competition), and lowers it for laggards (discouragement).
  - Aggregating gives an inverted-U across industries (UK, Aghion et al. 2005).
- **Import competition:** China's import competition raised innovation within European firms (about 15% of technology upgrading) but cut US patenting (about 40% of the 1999–2007 patent slowdown). The response depends on firm strength and distance to the frontier.
- **Market size:** 1% more potential market → 4–6% more new drugs. Export demand shocks raise patenting only among the most productive firms, with a 3–5 year lag.
- **Directed technical change:** prices redirect innovation. A 10% higher tax-inclusive fuel price → about 10% more clean and about 6% fewer dirty auto patents, with strong path dependence. The optimal climate policy combines carbon taxes with research subsidies.
- **Abuses of market power:** killer acquisitions (5.3–7.4% of pharma deals), political connections of market leaders, and size-dependent regulation all reduce innovation.

### Inferences
- Model competition through the gap between technology leader and laggard:
  - innovation effort rises with competition when firms are neck-and-neck;
  - it falls with competition when the gap is large;
  - the aggregate is an inverted-U.
- This reconciles D054 (Europe, upgrading) with D055 (US, contraction): the same shock helps strong or near-frontier firms and hurts weak ones.
- **Market size and prices redirect R&D across sectors.**
  - Elasticity of new products to market size: about 4–6.
  - Elasticity of patents in a technology line to its relative input price: about 1 for the clean line and about −0.6 for the dirty line.
  - Path dependence in knowledge stocks adds lock-in, so temporary policies can have permanent effects.
- **Competition-policy levers for players:**
  - merger control against killer acquisitions (about 6% of acquisitions; about 5% more projects continuing);
  - opening to foreign entrants (helps near-frontier sectors);
  - removing size-contingent regulation (+5.7% innovation);
  - anti-corruption (the political-connection leadership paradox).

### Gaps
- The published ABBGH (2005, QJE) sample details (often cited as about 311 firms in 17 industries, 1973–1994) and the location of the inverted-U peak were not verified; only the WP (UK listed firms, 1968–1997) was.
- Later tests that question the inverted-U, for example Hashmi (2013, REStat), which reports a mildly negative relation in US data, were not retrieved. Treat the inverted-U as debated.
- Bloom–Draca–Van Reenen's published share (14% vs the WP's 15%) was not verified.
- Acemoglu (2002) has no headline number. Quantitative directed-technical-change elasticities outside energy and pharma were not collected.

## 4. Institutions, political economy and innovation: incumbents blocking technology, property rights and patents, war and government missions, public R&D and basic research, R&D tax credits, immigration

### Takeaway
- **Incumbents and lobbies slow diffusion.** Where institutions make barriers cheap to erect, technologies with a close predecessor diffuse more slowly. This differential explains over 50% of the variance in technology adoption (20 technologies, 23 countries, about 215 years). The average country adopts a technology 45 years after its invention, and these lags explain at least 25% of income differences.
- **Patents shift the direction more than the rate of innovation** (Moser). Restrictive IP on cumulative research can reduce follow-on research by 20–30% (Celera), though gene patents overall had no measurable effect.
- **Mission and defense R&D crowds in private R&D and builds lasting clusters.**
  - Elasticity of private to government R&D: about 0.5 (10% → 5–6%).
  - WWII OSRD: $462m (1940–45 $), funding about 1 in 8 US patents at the peak; treated clusters had 40–50% more patents per year by 1970.
  - Apollo: modest local multipliers (about 0.3–0.4).
- **Public science feeds private patents:** NIH $10m → 2.3 net patents; DOE small-business grants, 1 patent → 3 more by others.
- **R&D tax credits work:**
  - user-cost elasticity about 1 or more long-run; −1.6 in UK medium firms; about 2.6 for UK SMEs;
  - a 17% rise in R&D for Canadian small firms;
  - relabeling is a real cost (China: 24.2% of reported R&D).
- **High-skill immigration has among the best-identified large effects:**
  - +31% US patenting in émigré fields;
  - 1 pp more immigrant college graduates → +9–18% patents per capita;
  - immigrants are 16% of US inventors and generate about 32% of innovation, including spillovers.
- **Taxes:** inventor location is elastic to top taxes (foreign superstars about 1). State patenting elasticity to the net-of-tax rate is 0.8–1.8 (personal) and 1.3–2.8 (corporate).

### Inferences
- **Political economy as a model mechanism.** Incumbent firm value falls with the creative-destruction hazard (D014). Incumbents therefore spend on lobbying or political connections (D064, D100) to:
  - raise entry costs;
  - slow adoption of substitute technologies (D070: the differential effect explains over 50% of adoption variance);
  - capture subsidies.

  Institutional quality (legislative effectiveness, democracy, rule of law; D070, D073) scales how much lobbying succeeds. Mokyr's "openness/culture" can be a slow-moving state variable that raises tolerance for creative destruction.
- **Mission R&D in wartime** (OSRD: about 1/8 of patents; clusters +20–50% for decades) and **defense R&D crowd-in** (elasticity about 0.5) give a realistic war-time R&D lever with persistent local effects. Space-race-style prestige programs have smaller multipliers (about 0.3–0.4).
- **Basic research as a separate stock** with larger spillovers (D082, D081, D050) maps onto Mokyr's propositional knowledge. A public-science budget raises the productivity of firms' applied R&D. Social returns are 3–4 times private (D083).
- **Tax credits:** the robust long-run elasticity is about 1, larger for constrained SMEs (about 2–2.6). Fiscal efficiency is about $1 of R&D per $1 forgone. About 24% of claims may be relabeling, depending on enforcement. Realistic sizes: 0–0.4% of GDP.
- **Immigration and talent:** among the largest identified effects. Émigré fields +31%; +9–18% patents per capita per pp of skilled-immigrant share; immigrants about 32% of innovation including spillovers. Talent moves with top taxes (foreign superstars, elasticity about 1). In a multi-nation game, attracting inventors from rivals is zero-sum for location but positive-sum through spillovers.

### Gaps
- Comin & Hobijn (2009) coefficient magnitudes (e.g., years of delay per unit of institutional quality) were not extracted, only the variance-share statement.
- Moser & San (2020) on the 1920s US quota acts and Doran & Yoon on immigration restrictions and invention were not retrieved. Waldinger (2016) and Iaria, Schwarz & Waldinger (2018, QJE) on WWI boycotts and frontier knowledge were not retrieved.
- Fleming et al. (2019, Science) on the share of US patents relying on federal research was not verified.
- The published REStud number for Azoulay et al. (possibly 2.7 rather than 2.3 patents per $10m) was not checked.
- The OECD 2026 release numbers were read from a search summary, not the page itself.
- No quantitative estimates were found of the effect of Mokyr-style "culture of growth" or openness variables on innovation rates in modern data, beyond historical case studies (D009–D013).

## 5. Misallocation and innovation; openness and trade as drivers of innovation

### Takeaway
- **Misallocation is large.** Equalizing marginal products to US levels would raise manufacturing TFP 30–50% in China and 40–60% in India. Weak plant life-cycle growth (India: little growth with age; Mexico: ×2 by age 40 vs ×8 in the US) costs about 25% of TFP.
- Misallocation interacts with innovation through selection: subsidizing incumbents can keep low-type firms alive (D030), and political connections reallocate toward unproductive leaders (D064).
- **Trade openness** works through four channels:
  - shorter adoption lags (D071);
  - escape-competition upgrading for strong firms (Europe–China: about 15% of upgrading);
  - discouragement for weak firms (US–China: about 40% of the patent slowdown);
  - larger market size for the most productive exporters (D056).

  Temporary protection can seed adoption whose effects fade over decades (D067).

### Cited Findings
**Catalog items D101–D104** (plus cross-references to D030, D041, D042, D054–D056, D064, D067, D071)

- **D101 — Hsieh & Klenow (2009, QJE): "Misallocation and Manufacturing TFP in China and India"** | Src: [NBER w13290](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13290) | Cov: manufacturing establishments in China, India and the US | Key: when capital and labor are reallocated "to equalize marginal products to the extent observed in the U.S., we calculate manufacturing TFP gains of 30-50% in China and 40-60% in India" | Rec: 2009 | Qual: sensitive to measurement error in revenue productivity (TFPR) dispersion | Use: **static misallocation wedge**. Nations with poor institutions run 30–60% below potential TFP for a given technology level A.
- **D102 — Bloom–Draca–Van Reenen vs Autor et al.: the competing China-shock results, side by side** | Src: [NBER w16717](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16717); [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20180481) | Key: Europe: +innovation within firms and reallocation, about 15% of 2000–07 upgrading. US: −patents, about 40% of the 1999–2007 patent slowdown, concentrated in less profitable, less capital-intensive firms | Use: target that the sign of trade-induced innovation depends on firm strength. Item for synthesis (detail in D054–D055).
- **D103 — Comin & Hobijn (2010 WP): trade openness and adoption lags** | Src: [NBER w12314](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12314) | Key: "Where adoption lags matter, they are largely determined by lack of educational attainment and lack of trade openness" (19 technologies, 21 countries, 1870–1998). - **D104 — Selection vs subsidies (Acemoglu et al. 2018) and life-cycle evidence (Hsieh & Klenow 2014) as misallocation–innovation links** | Src: [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20130470); [NBER w18133](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18133) | Key: taxing incumbents' continued operation brings about 1.4% welfare gains, while R&D subsidies to incumbents preserve low types. Weak growth after entry costs about 25% of TFP | Use: dynamic misallocation. Entry barriers and protection of incumbents lower both static TFP and innovation.

### Inferences
- A nation's effective productivity can be modeled as A × (1 − misallocation wedge), with the wedge at about 0.3–0.6 for distorted economies relative to the US benchmark (D101). The wedge depends on institutions and on how much incumbents are protected (D064, D030).
- Openness should carry two effects: a technology-transfer effect (shorter lags, D071 and D103) and a competition effect whose sign depends on the gap to the frontier (D052, D054, D055).
- **Gap-dependent sign rule for import competition:** near-frontier or profitable sectors upgrade (+); laggard or weak sectors contract their innovation (−).
- Temporary protection (D067) is a legitimate early-game lever, with fading benefits after about 50 years.

### Gaps
- Hsieh & Klenow's later "Reallocation Myth" view and the debate over measurement error in revenue-productivity (TFPR) dispersion (e.g., Bils, Klenow & Ruane) were not retrieved, so the 30–60% wedge may be an upper bound.
- Peters (2020) on endogenous misallocation through markups in Indonesia was not verified.
- No direct estimates were collected of trade-liberalization elasticities of patents beyond the China shock (e.g., Bustos 2011 on Argentina, Coelli et al. 2022 on tariff cuts).

## 6. Which policy levers have the best-identified effects on innovation rates, and how large are they?

### Takeaway
The best-identified, most robust levers, with magnitudes usable as game parameters:
1. **R&D tax credits:** long-run user-cost elasticity of 1 or more; −1.6 for UK medium firms; 2 or more for constrained SMEs; about $1 of R&D per $1 forgone; about 24% relabeling risk.
2. **Skilled immigration and talent:** +9–18% patents per capita per pp of skilled-immigrant share; +31% in émigré fields; foreign superstar inventor location elasticity of about 1.
3. **Public or defense R&D:** private crowd-in elasticity of about 0.5; +0.08 pp TFP growth per +1 pp of defense R&D/VA; WWII mission clusters +20–50% patents for decades.
4. **Basic science grants:** NIH $10m → 2.3 net patents; DOE small-business grants, each induced patent → 3 more via spillovers.
5. **Market size and prices (directed technical change):** elasticity of new products to market size 4–6; clean-patent elasticity to relative input price about 1.
6. **Taxes on inventors and corporations:** patent elasticities to net-of-tax rates of 0.8–2.8 (mostly quantity and location).
7. **Competition and regulation:** removing a 50-employee regulatory threshold → +5.7% innovation; competition effects are gap-dependent (inverted-U); killer acquisitions are about 6% of pharma deals.

Evidence on mission-oriented policy and IP reform is weakest (Bloom–Van Reenen–Williams rate both "Low" or "Unknown").

    - lowers the R&D user cost, with elasticity about 1 (up to 2–2.6 for small, constrained firms);
    - each $1 spent yields about $1 of R&D;
    - a "relabeling leak" of about 24% falls with enforcement or institutional quality;
    - realistic size 0–0.4% of GDP.
  - **(b) Public basic-science budget:** raises a basic-knowledge stock that multiplies applied R&D productivity (Mokyr; Akcigit–Hanley–Serrano-Velarde). Conversion targets: about 0.23 private patents per $1m (NIH); spillover multiplier about 4 (DOE).
  - **(c) Mission or defense R&D:** crowd-in elasticity about 0.5; persistent clusters (+20–50% patents decades later); possible during wars.
  - **(d) Immigration and talent openness:** +9–18% patents per pp of skilled share; brain-drain or brain-gain events of ±30% in affected fields; foreign inventors' location elasticity to top taxes about 1.
  - **(e) Competition and antitrust:** gap-dependent effect on innovation; merger control; removing size thresholds (+5.7%).
  - **(f) IP strength:** mostly shifts the direction of innovation across sectors (Moser). Restrictive IP on foundational knowledge costs 20–30% of follow-on work.
  - **(g) Social insurance:** raises tolerance for creative destruction and reduces Luddite or Swing-type unrest.
  - **(h) Anti-lobbying institutions:** reduce the adoption penalty for substitute technologies (Comin–Hobijn).
- **Time-to-effect matters:**
  - tax credits work in the short run (3–4 years per the toolkit);
  - export-demand shocks raise patenting after 3–5 years;
  - mission clusters build over 20–30 years;
  - STEM supply works in the long run;

- I did not verify the elasticity of scientist wages to R&D subsidies (Goolsbee 1998), which matters for diminishing returns.

## 7. Objective-level synthesis: what is the current consensus, and which stylized-fact targets should a "productivity growth through technological innovation" model match?

### Takeaway
The consensus after the 2025 prize:
1. Long-run growth in frontier economies (1–2%/yr) comes from innovation, mostly by incumbents improving their own products (about 60–70%). Creative destruction (about 25%) and new entrants (about 20–50% of growth for firms aged 0–5) matter far beyond their employment shares.
2. Innovation requires an open knowledge system linking science and technique, plus upper-tail technical skill (Mokyr).
3. Competition has a gap-dependent, non-monotonic effect.
4. Incumbents and lobbies systematically try to block creative destruction; institutions determine whether they succeed.
5. Advanced economies show declining dynamism since about 2000, rising concentration, frontier–laggard divergence and slower productivity growth. Reduced knowledge diffusion is the leading single explanation.
6. Social returns to R&D exceed private returns by about 3–4×, so well-designed tax credits, public basic research, mission R&D and skilled immigration raise innovation with well-identified magnitudes.

### Cited Findings
- **Growth decomposition:** about 25% creative destruction, the rest mostly incumbents' own innovation — [GHK 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22953). 60/27/13 (own / new varieties / creative destruction), with firms aged 0–5 producing almost half of growth — [Klenow & Li 2021](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27015).
- **Entry/exit share:** about one quarter of TFP growth; reallocation about half (1997–2015) — [Nobel background](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2025/10/advanced-economicsciencesprize2025.pdf).
- **Dynamism decline (US):** startup rate 13.71% (1978) → 8.50% (2019); reallocation 30.06% (1978–89 average) → 23.08% (2008–19 average) — [BDS 2023 CSVs, my calculation](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/bds/tables/time-series/2023/bds2023.csv).
- **Divergence:** frontier about 2%/yr vs laggards under 0.1%/yr (manufacturing MFP); 2.3 pp/yr divergence associated with −0.6 pp aggregate productivity growth — [Andrews, Criscuolo & Gal](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/dp1645.pdf).
- **Diffusion:** adoption lag of 45 years on average — [Comin & Hobijn 2010](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.100.5.2031). Lobby-related differential effects explain over 50% of adoption variance — [Comin & Hobijn 2009](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11022).
- **Research productivity:** −5.1%/yr since the 1930s (already in the game) — [Bloom et al. 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20180338).
- **Social vs private return:** 57.7% vs 13.6% — [Lucking, Bloom & Van Reenen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24622).

### Inferences

**Mechanism order** (from minimal to full), compatible with the existing Jones/Bloom-calibrated A:
1. Split the Jones-generated A growth into channels (own / creative destruction / variety) with T2 shares. Add a creative-destruction hazard per sector that moves market share and profits between firms (Aghion–Howitt).
2. Add firm entry and exit with T3 targets; entrants carry the higher-spillover, radical innovations (Akcigit–Kerr).
3. Add a gap-dependent competition effect (escape vs discouragement; inverted-U) and leader-to-laggard diffusion (Akcigit–Ates). With light-speed delays, diffusion between star systems is a natural extension of Comin–Hobijn adoption lags.
4. Add political economy: incumbent lobbying against entry and substitute technologies, scaled by institutional quality (Comin–Hobijn; Akcigit–Baslandze–Lotti; Mokyr's Cardwell's Law). An "openness" state variable lets leadership erode endogenously.
5. Add policy levers with the D105 magnitudes and time lags.

### Gaps
- The cross-country (non-US) decomposition of growth into own innovation, creative destruction and new varieties is not available in what I collected. GHK and Klenow–Li are US-only.
- Quantitative estimates of how culture or openness (Mokyr) moves modern innovation rates are lacking; the evidence is historical and mostly correlational.
- 2025–26 academic critiques of the Aghion–Howitt policy conclusions (e.g., industrial policy for Europe) were not found in this session.
