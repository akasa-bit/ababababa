## 1. Cross-country innovation inputs, outputs and TFP versus income level (income-quintile targets)

### Takeaway
Innovation inputs rise much faster than income. Across 2015–2023 country means, the elasticity of researchers per million with respect to GDP per capita is 1.35, resident patents per million 1.61, R&D/GDP 0.71, and high-tech export share 0.44. Median R&D/GDP is about 0.15–0.26% in the bottom three quintiles and 1.86% in the top. TFP relative to the US (PWT ctfp) rises far less steeply: elasticity 0.30, medians 0.35 (Q1) to 0.85 (Q5). TFP shows no scale effect with population: elasticity −0.004 once income is controlled for.

### Inferences
- The game's S = population × f(income) should make researchers per million rise with income with an elasticity of about 1.3–1.4 (E003). That is a factor of about 100 between the Q1 and Q5 medians, not proportional to income. The R&D/GDP curve is convex: flat at about 0.15–0.26% through Q1–Q3, then 0.66% (Q4) and 1.86% (Q5).
- Combined with E014–E016, the data imply that TFP gaps (2.4× from Q1 to Q5) are far smaller than research-input gaps (117×). In a Jones model without spillovers, A_i ∝ S_i^(1/β) gives a Q5/Q1 per-capita TFP ratio of about (117)^(1/3) ≈ 4.9 at equal populations, and it gives large countries an advantage, so China and India would sit near the frontier. Both contradict E014–E016. This is the strongest quantitative argument for adding cross-nation diffusion from a shared frontier.

## 2. Technology adoption lags, intensity gaps and diffusion S-curves

### Takeaway
Across countries and technologies, the average adoption lag is 41–45 years. It has fallen steeply with invention date: roughly 1.1% per year of invention vintage, which is about 10% per decade. By 2026 data the average fell from 50 years for pre-1950 technologies to 15 years for post-2000 ones, and it fell 5.7% per year for post-1990 technologies. 4G's lag is 2 years against 117–130 years for spindles. The intensity of use in non-Western or developing countries is persistently about half (47–53%) of advanced levels, and the intensity gap explains the 20th-century divergence. Mobile generations are the exception: developing countries leapfrog. Within-country S-curves are fast: the US 10%→50% median is 9 years, recent technologies take 2–8 years, and the median logistic slope is 0.12 per year.

## 3. Distance, borders and time in knowledge flows (relevant to light-speed information)

### Takeaway
Knowledge flows are strongly localized, but mostly through borders and early-life frictions rather than pure distance. Citation-flow distance elasticities are small (−0.03), while borders block most flows: about 80% at regional borders and 81–89% at national borders. The half-distance of R&D spillovers is about 800–1,900 km (Keller), and spatial technology-adoption interactions decay 73% per 1,000 km (CDRH). Localization weakens over time and with the age of knowledge: Keller finds at least a two-thirds fall from the 1970s to the 1990s, CDRH find distance effects vanish as diffusion proceeds, and Li finds border and distance effects shrink as patents age.

- Keller's and CDRH's results that localization falls with better communication suggest the game's light-speed limit is the binding "distance" cost. Beyond τ, an extra decay per light-year should be mild.

## 4. Research productivity by field; R&D depreciation and gestation lags

### Takeaway
Research productivity falls at about 5% per year for the US aggregate (β ≈ 3.1), 6.8% for Moore's law (β = 0.2), 3.7–5.5% for agriculture (β 2.2–3.3), and 3.5–10% for medicine. ### Inferences
- Frontier TFP growth for a 2300–2800 game: historical decade averages for the frontier lie in [0.5, 3.2]%/yr, centred on 1.0–1.5% (BCL 1890–2019 US mean 1.56; Fernald 1948–2025 1.19). Decade-to-decade "waves" of ±1 pp (1930s–60s high, 1973–95 low, 1995–2004 burst) are the right magnitude for technology waves.
- Catch-up should produce conditional convergence of about 2%/yr toward a country-specific steady state while keeping unconditional convergence weak (|β| < 0.5%/yr). Steady-state TFP gaps are set by intensity and absorptive capacity, not by lags alone.

## 6. How games and simulations represent technology progress and diffusion (design patterns, E082–E104)

### Takeaway
Commercial strategy games almost all use discrete research trees: era or tier costs plus explicit catch-up subsidies. Civ IV gives up to +30% research rate in proportion to the share of contacted teams that know a tech. EU4 gives −5% cost per neighbour with the tech (capped at −75%), a +10% ahead-of-time penalty per year, and institutions that spread province to province. Victoria 3 has literacy-driven tech "spread" with randomness and an innovation cap. HOI4 applies a 200%-per-year ahead-of-time penalty, Stellaris draws 3 semi-random options with weights that include allies' techs, and Factorio uses infinite research with geometric costs. Agent-based macro models (the Dosi et al. K+S model) instead use stochastic, R&D-driven innovation and imitation draws embodied in capital vintages. The Victoria 3 and EU4 spread rules, the Civ IV "known by contacts" subsidy, and the economists' lag-plus-intensity and meeting models translate most directly to a continuous model with light-speed delays.

### Inferences
- Realism ranking for a continuous, light-delay economy:
  - Good fit: Victoria 3-style spread driven by absorptive capacity (literacy or human capital, noise, saturation for leaders). The Civ IV "share of contacts who know it" subsidy translates as: share of nations whose knowledge has reached you by light. EU4's capped neighbour discount, the economists' lag-plus-intensity model (E098) and meeting-based diffusion (E099–E100) also fit.
  - Partial fit: EU4 institutions (waves spreading by adjacency, with adoption costs scaling with development) and Factorio-style geometric cost growth (equivalent to rising difficulty).
  - Poor fit: ahead-of-time penalties anchored to historical dates (EU4, HOI4), because no history exists for 2300–2800, and Stellaris random cards in a continuous model, unless reinterpreted as stochastic sector-shock draws.
- Commercial games subsidize laggards much more generously than the data show: EU4 up to −75%, Civ IV +30%. Empirically, foreign R&D is worth about 37–74% of domestic R&D within about 7,500 km (Keller), and only 9–12% of citation flow crosses national borders (Peri, Li). Strong catch-up is therefore a gameplay choice rather than an empirical fact.

## 7. Synthesis: what to target, which parameters to use, and which mechanisms fit a continuous model with light-speed information

### Takeaway
The current per-nation Jones model with no spillovers would fail three robust facts:
1. TFP gaps are about 2.4× from Q1 to Q5, while research inputs differ by about 117× (E003, E014).
2. TFP levels show no country-size effect (E016).
3. Adoption lags of about 41–45 years historically, shrinking to 2–8 years for networked technologies, with intensity about 50% of advanced levels (E020–E031).

A minimal fix keeps own research (β ≈ 3, sector-specific optional) and adds an absorption term from a light-delayed foreign frontier. The absorption rate depends on absorptive capacity (income or human capital) and on border or polity multipliers, and an intensity factor caps the level a nation can reach. Calibrate these to the quintile bands and to 2%/yr conditional convergence.

### Cited Findings
- Proposed target bands, all from items above:
  - TFP relative to frontier: Q1 0.27–0.43, Q2 0.43–0.67, Q3 0.48–0.73, Q4 0.63–0.78, Q5 0.79–0.93 (E014). Elasticity with income 0.30 (E015). Population elasticity about 0 (E016).
  - Researchers per million (E003): Q1 29–72, Q2 57–278, Q3 288–1,460, Q4 612–2,980, Q5 3,150–5,780. R&D/GDP: Q1–Q3 0.07–0.67%, Q4 0.37–1.17%, Q5 0.98–2.94% (E002).
  - Resident patents per million: Q1 0.18–1.06 … Q5 34.6–215 (E005).
  - Adoption lags: mean 41–45 years for old technologies (E020, E027); lag elasticity to invention date −1.1%/yr (E024, E029); recent networked technologies 2–8 years (E028); income ladder for networked technologies with lags versus the top quintile of 10–17 years (Q1), 8–12 (Q2), 5–9 (Q3), 3–4 (Q4) (E033–E035).
  - Intensity: developing/advanced 0.47–0.53 (E025, E027). Country fixed effects explain about 28% of lag variance, technology effects about 58% (E030).
  - Diffusion S-curve: logistic slope 0.055–0.218 per year (median 0.123) (E038); 10→50 in 4–6 years for mobile and 6–10 for internet (E037).
  - Frontier TFP growth: 0.5–3.2%/yr by decade, long-run 1.2–1.6% (E068–E070), annual shock SD about 1.5–1.8 pp (E069).
  - Development accounting: TFP share 0.6–0.86 (E073–E076).
  - Conditional convergence 1.7–2.4%/yr (E077); unconditional β 0.3–0.5%/yr since 1990 (E080), with a wide band given E079.
  - Research productivity decline 4–5.5%/yr aggregate (β 2.4–3.3) (E054, E059); about 5% in Germany and about 24% in China (E060).
- Proposed parameters: R&D-to-knowledge gestation lag about 2 years (E065); knowledge depreciation 15–35%/yr by sector (E063–E064); border pass-through 0.11–0.21 of citation flow (E047, E052); a weak extra decay beyond the information delay (E052–E053); spillover weight relative to own R&D: foreign R&D worth 0.37–0.74 of domestic within transoceanic or nearby range (E045), or a 2–3% response in neighbours against 80–90% at home (E050).

### Inferences
- Suggested mechanism, continuous time. For nation i and sector s:
  dA_is/dt = α S_is A_is^(1−β_s) + κ_i · Σ_j w_ij · [θ_i · A_js(t − τ_ij) − A_is(t)]⁺,
  where τ_ij = distance_ij/c (years; light delay) + D_0 (institutional or absorption lag, about 2–8 years for networked technologies), w_ij is a contact or polity weight (Peri/Li border multipliers; Civ IV-style share of contacts), θ_i ≤ 1 is the intensity ceiling (about 0.5 in poor nations, 1 in rich ones), and κ_i is the absorption speed, rising with income or human capital (Comin–Hobijn; Victoria 3 literacy). Calibrate κ so the half-life of a gap to θ·A_frontier is about 35 years (2%/yr, Barro). Fit θ_i(income) so ctfp falls in the E014 quintile bands. This removes the scale effect (E016), because a large nation's own S matters less once foreign knowledge is available.
- Waves: a few discrete "technology generations" (GPTs) per century could each raise the frontier ceiling and diffuse logistically (slope about 0.12/yr, faster for networked technologies). Keep the frontier decade averages within 0.5–3.2%/yr and the ±1 pp decade swings seen in BCL and Fernald. Generations can show leapfrogging, where intensity exceeds 1 for low-infrastructure technologies (E031).
- Creative destruction and adoption costs: K+S-style stochastic R&D success (ζ ≈ 0.3) with Beta(3,3) proportional draws on ±15% gives firm-level dispersion, and embodiment via machine vintages (lifetime about 19 periods) produces diffusion through investment. At nation-sector level, a reduced form is a Poisson arrival of innovations with size draws.
- Light-speed specific: Li's age effect and the finding that localization fades over time (CDRH, Keller) imply that once knowledge arrives, the remaining distance penalty should decay with knowledge age. A simple rule: the effective weight rises from w_ij × (border factor) toward w_ij as the age of knowledge since arrival grows (half-life of order a decade). This reproduces "borders matter for new ideas, not old ones".
- Game-design calibration: commercial catch-up subsidies (EU4 −75% cap, Civ IV +30%) are larger than empirical spillovers. If fun requires strong catch-up, use the absorption speed κ rather than cost discounts, so measured TFP gaps stay inside the quintile bands.

### Gaps
- No direct empirical estimate exists for knowledge diffusion over delays longer than Earth-scale communication delays (all evidence comes from km-scale distances or pre-telecom eras such as spindles and ships, with lags of 100+ years). Using historical pre-telegraph lags (E023: ships, spindles, railways 69–130 years) as analogues for multi-decade light delays is an inference, not evidence.
