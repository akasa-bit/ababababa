## Q1. Kondratiev's original data and claims; later theorists (Schumpeter 1939, Mandel, Freeman & Louçã, Perez 2002)

### Takeaway
Kondratiev (1925/26; English 1935) claimed ~50-year waves mostly from detrended price, interest-rate and some production series for England, France and the US from the late 1780s to 1920; later theorists re-explained them as innovation clusters (Schumpeter), profit-rate dynamics with exogenous upturns (Mandel), or successive techno-economic paradigms with installation/deployment phases (Freeman & Louçã; Perez's five surges since 1771). These are interpretive frameworks; none is mainstream economics, but Perez's "installation → financial bubble → turning point → deployment" sequence is widely used in innovation studies and policy.

### Inferences
- The original Kondratiev evidence is predominantly nominal (prices, interest rates); production evidence was thin and trend-dependent. A game should not import "50-year cycles" as a deterministic clock.
- The theorists agree on one robust qualitative sequence that does have empirical support elsewhere (Q3): a new technology cluster → heavy investment/financial speculation → crisis/reallocation → broad deployment and productivity gains. This is better modelled as stochastic GPT arrivals plus slow diffusion than as a sine wave.
- Perez's five "big-bang" dates (1771 … 1971) give intervals of roughly 33–63 years (mean ≈50) if the standard intermediate dates (Gaps) are accepted; that is a usable prior for the mean GPT inter-arrival time, with high variance.

### Gaps
- Perez's intermediate big-bang events and years (commonly 1829 "Rocket"/Liverpool–Manchester railway; 1875 Carnegie Bessemer plant; 1908 Model-T; 1971 Intel microprocessor) are my recollection of Perez (2002) Table 2.1 and were not re-verified this session.
- Schumpeter's own Kondratieff dating (often cited as 1787–1842, 1843–1897, 1898–) not verified.
- Freeman & Louçã dating table; Mensch's basic-innovation cluster years; the Freeman–Clark–Soete critique content; Rosenberg–Frischtak text: not retrieved.

## Q2. Empirical tests of long waves and innovation clustering: what holds up, and the current verdict

### Takeaway
Spectral, filter and wavelet tests give mixed results: long swings are most robust in price series (US/UK/France 1791–2012) and are claimed for world GDP growth 1870–2007 (≈52–53-year peak, significance 4–5%), but national output series mostly fail (Solomou; van Ewijk; Diebolt & Doliger; Metz, who renounced his own 1992 positive finding; Focacci 2023). For innovation clustering, the best test (Silverberg & Verspagen 2003 Poisson regression) supports only overdispersion ("bursty" arrivals), not periodic clusters. Mainstream verdict: no reliable, regular 50-year output cycle; irregular long swings and GPT-driven "eras" yes.

- For the game: implement GPT arrivals as a stochastic, overdispersed process with slow diffusion and complementary investment; the resulting simulated TFP path will show irregular long swings (as in C024) that can be compared against C015/C020 phase contrasts without forcing periodicity.

## Q3. GPTs: theory and evidence; diffusion times; timing of productivity effects (years from invention to peak contribution)

### Takeaway
GPT theory (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg; Helpman & Trajtenberg; David 1990) and growth-accounting history agree that the productivity payoff of a GPT arrives decades after invention and is preceded by a slowdown/J-curve while complements are built. Quantified: steam's contribution peaked at 0.38 pp/yr of British labour-productivity growth in 1850–70, roughly 80–100 years after Watt; electricity peaked at 0.98 pp/yr in the US in 1919–29, ≈40 years after Edison/Pearl Street (1879–82) and ≈25–35 years after the 1894 "1% diffusion" date; ICT peaked at ≈1.8 pp/yr in 1996–2001, ≈25–30 years after the 1971 microprocessor. Cross-country adoption lags average 42–47 years and have fallen steadily for newer technologies.

### Cited Findings
- **C028 — Bresnahan & Trajtenberg (1995 *J. Econometrics*; cited as 1996): GPT criteria** · Src: as summarised in [Jovanovic & Rousseau 2005, NBER w11093](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11093) · Key: (1) pervasiveness — spreads to most sectors; (2) improvement — keeps lowering user costs; (3) innovation spawning — makes it easier to invent new products/processes · Status: mainstream definition · Model: GPT object needs (sector coverage, own-improvement rate, spillover to idea production).

### Inferences
- **Invention → peak-contribution lags (stylized targets)**: steam ≈80–100 years (Watt 1769 → peak 1850–70, C035); electricity ≈40 years from first central station (1882) or ≈25–35 years from 1% diffusion (1894) to the 1919–29 peak (C030, C034); ICT ≈25–30 years from the 1971 microprocessor to the 1996–2004 surge (C038, C030). The lag shortens across GPTs, consistent with Comin–Mestieri's ≈0.76–1.1%/yr decline in log adoption lags per year of invention date (C045).
- **Peak magnitude**: ≈0.4 (steam), ≈1.0 (electricity), ≈1.8 (ICT) pp/yr added to labour-productivity growth; elevated contribution lasts ≈10–20 years (1850–70; 1919–29; 1996–2004). The ICT peak was short and US-centred (Q4, C054).
- **Mechanism**: the measured-TFP dip/J-curve (C040, C039, C029) and dispersion-then-selection (C042) are well supported; but per-adopter gains can be fast (C043), so the long aggregate lag comes mainly from slow adoption and complementary investment.
- **Inter-nation diffusion**: the game currently has no diffusion; a two-margin model (lag D falling from ~100+ to ~6 years over two centuries of invention dates; intensity gap persisting at ~0.5 for laggards) is the best-supported structure. With light-speed information, the communication delay sets a hard floor on D, but historical D (decades) is dominated by absorptive capacity, not information delay.
- Implied GPT arrival rate: steam (1769), electricity (1879–94), ICT (1971), AI (2010s–2020s) → ≈4 major GPTs in ~250 years (one per ~60–75 years), matching the Perez-surge spacing (Q1).

## Q4. Historical productivity growth by era, and long historical datasets

### Takeaway
US TFP growth shows "one big wave": ≈0.5–1.0%/yr in 1890–1920, ≈1.9–2.3%/yr in 1920–70 (peaking in the 1930s–40s), ≈0.5–1.1%/yr after 1970 with a short ICT revival (≈1.8–2.0%/yr in 1996–2004); other advanced economies had the same big wave later (after WWII) and a much smaller ICT wave. Across 23 OECD countries 1890–2024, decade-average TFP growth has mean ≈1.4%/yr, SD ≈1.5 pp, P10 ≈−0.3, P90 ≈3.2 (computed here). Several free long-run datasets support these targets.

## Q5. AI as a GPT: macro estimates, micro evidence, and the growth-explosion debate

### Takeaway
Ten-year macro estimates span two orders of magnitude: ≈0.07 pp/yr TFP (Acemoglu: ≤0.66% cumulative over 10 years) to ≈1.3–1.8 pp/yr labour productivity (Aghion–Bunel historical analogy; Goldman Sachs 1.5; Anthropic 1.8 upper bound without adoption frictions); central official/think-tank estimates cluster at ≈0.2–0.7 pp/yr (OECD, Aghion–Bunel median 0.68, PWBM peak 0.2). Micro RCTs show large task-level gains (14–56%) concentrated among less-experienced workers, but also a 19% slowdown for experts in mature codebases and precise zero earnings/hours effects in Danish administrative data. Explosive-growth (>20–30%/yr) scenarios are a minority but serious research programme; standard tests (Nordhaus) find no sign of approaching singularity.

### Inferences
- **Range for a decade-long AI boost (pp/yr)**: lower bound ≈0.07 (Acemoglu); central ≈0.2–0.7 (PWBM peak 0.2; OECD TFP 0.3–0.7; Aghion–Bunel median 0.68; French commission 0.7); upper ≈0.8–1.8 (Aghion–Bunel analogy 0.8–1.3; GS 1.5; OECD LP high 1.3; Anthropic 1.8). Historical GPT peaks (Q3: 0.4 / 1.0 / 1.8 pp) sit inside this range, so AI-as-GPT at ≈ICT strength (≈1–1.8 pp for ~10 years) is the most history-consistent "strong" scenario.
- **Micro → macro gap**: task-level savings 15–56% (C076–C080) shrink to ≈1% level effects (C074) and null wage/hour effects (C082) because exposure × adoption × intensity is small and complements are missing — the same J-curve as electricity/ICT.
- **Heterogeneity**: gains largest for low-experience workers; negative for experts on some tasks (C078, C081) → AI raises low-A workers/firms more (within-nation convergence), while national exposure and preparedness differ (C069, C072).
- **Explosive growth**: requires automating most tasks including idea production (C084, C087, C088); empirical singularity indicators are not flashing (C085). For a 2300–2800 game, explosive phases are a legitimate scenario switch but should be gated by automation share ≥50–70% and limited by non-automatable bottlenecks.

## Q6. Productivity slowdown since ~2005 and mismeasurement

### Takeaway
The post-2004/05 slowdown is real and broad (dozens of countries; US TFP ≈0.5%/yr in 2005–19 vs ≈1.8–2.0%/yr in 1996–2004); mismeasurement of digital goods explains at most a small part (Syverson; Byrne, Fernald & Reinsdorf). The best recent survey finds no single cause — a 0.8–1.8 pp labour-productivity slowdown explained by several modest factors. US TFP growth has recovered to ≈1.1–1.3%/yr in 2020–25, but utilization-adjusted TFP fell over the year to 2026Q2, so no AI-driven break is yet visible in aggregate data.

### Cited Findings
- **C090 — Syverson (2017), "Challenges to mismeasurement explanations for the US productivity slowdown", *JEP* 31(2):165–186 (NBER w21974)** · Src: [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21974); [Brookings slides](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/syverson.pdf); replication [openICPSR](https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/113991/version/V1/view) · Key: slowdown since 2004 implies ≥$2.7 trillion of "missing output" (slides: ≈$3 trillion); the largest estimate of digital surplus is <1/3 of that; slowdown occurs in dozens of countries and is unrelated to ICT intensity; ICT industries' true growth would need to be several times measured growth; GDI > GDP since 2004 began before the slowdown and reflects capital income · Status: mainstream consensus · Model: treat the slowdown as real.
- **C091 — Byrne, Fernald & Reinsdorf (2016), "Does the United States have a productivity slowdown or a measurement problem?", *BPEA* Spring 2016** · Src: [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/bpea-articles/does-the-united-states-have-a-productivity-slowdown-or-a-measurement-problem/); [FRBSF WP 2016-03](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/working-papers/2016/03/does-the-united-states-have-a-productivity-slowdown-or-a-measurement-problem/) · Key: little evidence the slowdown comes from growing mismeasurement; IT-hardware mismeasurement significant before 2004, and its productivity effect larger in 1995–2004 than after (domestic production fell); adjustments make the labour-productivity slowdown worse, TFP effect muted; free online services largely non-market · Status: mainstream · Model: mismeasurement correction would steepen, not erase, the 2005 break.
- **C092 — Goldin, Koutroumpis, Lafond & Winkler (2024), "Why is productivity slowing down?", *JEL* 62(1):196–268** · Src: [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20221543); [INET Oxford](https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/news/why-has-productivity-slowed-down) · Cov: 5 advanced economies, post-2005 vs preceding decade · Key: labour-productivity slowdown of 0.8–1.8 pp; no single dominant cause; contributors: mismeasurement, weaker capital deepening, reduced intangible spillovers, slower trade growth, slower allocative-efficiency gains (sectoral reallocation and human capital in some countries); most of the slowdown is lower TFP and capital-deepening contributions, manufacturing largest sectoral share · Status: mainstream survey · Model: slowdown = sum of several small frictions, not a single "ideas running out" switch.

- **C095 — Crafts & Mills (2017) trend view (see C056)** · Src: [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/nierev/v242y2017ipr60-r67_15.html) · Key: smooth trend decline 1.5 → 1.0%/yr over 50 years, with large unpredictable deviations · Model: slowdown partly a regime shift, partly a long trend decline.
- **C096 — BCL decade data (from C053, computed here)** · Src: [BCL v2.7](http://www.longtermproductivity.com/download/BCLDatabase_online_v2.7.xlsx) · Key: 2010s TFP growth: US 0.86, UK −0.18, France 0.21, Germany 0.68, Japan 0.58 %/yr — the slowdown is sharper outside the US · Model: target for multi-nation slowdown dispersion.
- **C097 — Gordon headwinds and forecast (C049)** · Src: [Gordon 2016 AER P&P](https://gordon.economics.northwestern.edu/files/2021/04/PerspRiseFall.pdf) · Key: forecast output per hour 1.20%/yr 2015–2040; headwinds: inequality, slowing educational attainment, demographics, rising debt/GDP; R&D-intensive industries (≈80% of corporate R&D) are <5% of value added · Status: techno-pessimist (contested) · Model: pessimistic baseline.

### Inferences
- Mainstream view: the slowdown is real, multi-causal, and began before the 2008 crisis (≈2004–06). Mismeasurement and J-curve corrections are each ≤≈0.3–0.4 pp/yr against a ≈1.5 pp/yr slowdown.
- The slowdown fits the GPT-era pattern: ICT's peak contribution faded after ≈10 years (1996–2004), as electricity's did after 1929 and steam's after 1870. A game GPT whose contribution decays ~10–20 years after peak reproduces it.
- Signs to watch for an AI-driven break: utilization-adjusted TFP sustained >1.5%/yr for several years (C054); none visible as of 2026Q2.

## Q7 (cross-cutting). What current evidence supports vs rejects, and stylized-fact targets for modelling "productivity growth through technological innovation"

### Takeaway
Supported (mainstream): episodic GPTs with decades-long diffusion, a J-curve/initial slowdown, peak contributions of ≈0.4–1.8 pp/yr lasting ≈10–20 years, staggered international diffusion with adoption lags averaging ≈42–47 years that fall for newer technologies, and an overdispersed (bursty) arrival of major innovations. Rejected or not supported: regular 50-year Kondratiev cycles in real output/TFP and depression-triggered innovation clusters. Contested: world-GDP spectral K-waves, Perez-type phase regularity, AI's decade effect (0.07–1.8 pp/yr), and explosive growth.

### Cited Findings
- **C099 — Verdict on regular long waves in output** — rejected by Solomou ([CUP](https://booksinbelgium.be/nl/b/phases-of-economic-growth-1850-1973-solomos-solomou-9780521389044)), Metz's own retraction ([Korotayev & Tsirel](https://www.rhuthmos.eu/IMG/pdf/Korotayev_Kondratieff.pdf)), Focacci 2023 ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7099/11/9/231)); supported only by Korotayev & Tsirel's 52–53-year world-GDP peak at 4–5% significance ([PDF](https://www.rhuthmos.eu/IMG/pdf/Korotayev_Kondratieff.pdf)); status "not accepted by most academic economists" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondratiev_wave)).
- **C100 — Verdict on long swings in prices** — supported for US/UK/France wholesale prices 1791–2012 by wavelets ([Gallegati et al. 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/cliomt/v11y2017i1d10.1007_s11698-015-0137-y.html)); linked to war severity ([Goldstein](https://p2pfoundation.net/Prosperity_and_War_in_the_Modern_Age)).
- **C101 — Verdict on innovation clustering** — only overdispersion ([Silverberg & Verspagen 2003](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/portal/en/publications/breaking-the-waves-a-poisson-regression-approach-to-schumpeterian-clustering-of-basic-innovations(57ffd393-ee18-4351-853f-66ed97177dea).html)); text-based breakthrough waves in the 1880s, 1920s–30s, post-1980 ([Kelly et al. 2021](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aeri.20190499)).
- **C102 — Verdict on GPT lags and J-curves** — supported ([Crafts 2004](https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp7503.pdf); [Crafts 2002](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/3142.html); [Jovanovic & Rousseau 2005](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11093); [Brynjolfsson et al. 2021](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mac.20180386)); partially contested at plant level ([Fiszbein et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28076)).
- **C103 — Verdict on the "one big wave"** — supported ([Gordon 2016](https://gordon.economics.northwestern.edu/files/2021/04/PerspRiseFall.pdf); [Bergeaud et al.](https://publications.banque-france.fr/sites/default/files/medias/documents/document-de-travail_475_2014.pdf)); 1930s as the single best decade contested ([Bakker, Crafts & Woltjer via VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/onwards-and-upwards-american-productivity-growth-during-great-depression)).
- **C104 — Verdict on international diffusion** — adoption lags converge while intensity diverges ([Comin & Mestieri 2018](https://crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/TAE-1.pdf)); lags explain ≥25% of income differences ([Comin & Hobijn 2010](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-technology-adoption-affects-global-economies)).
- **C105 — Verdict on AI's decade effect** — range 0.07 ([Acemoglu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487)) to 1.3 ([Aghion & Bunel](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/AI-and-Growth-Aghion-Bunel.pdf)) to 1.8 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/estimating-productivity-gains)) pp/yr; population-level earnings/hours effects ≈0 so far ([Humlum & Vestergaard](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/33777.html)).
- **C106 — Verdict on explosive growth** — not supported by current indicators ([Nordhaus 2021](https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cowles-discussion-paper-series/2462)); conditional scenarios in [Davidson 2021](https://www.openphilanthropy.org/wp-content/uploads/Could-Advanced-AI-Drive-Explosive-Economic-Growth_.pdf), [Epoch GATE](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/ai-and-explosive-growth-redux), [Trammell & Korinek](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31815/w31815.pdf).
- **C107 — Real-interest-rate and reallocation signatures of GPT eras** — real rates ≈3 pp higher in 1894–1930 and 1971–2003 than in 1931–70; patents, trademarks, IPOs, entry/exit higher ([Jovanovic & Rousseau](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11093)).
- **C108 — GPT own-price improvement rates** — steam hp cost (current £) 33.5 (1760) → 4.0 (1910), steam-power TFP 0–3.5%/yr ([Crafts 2004](https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp7503.pdf)); IT prices falling ≥100× faster than electricity's ([Jovanovic & Rousseau](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11093)); computing power ≈55%/yr 1940–2001 ([Nordhaus 2001](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=285168)).

### Inferences
Suggested targets for the game (to fix from raw data before mechanisms):
1. **Frontier TFP growth by era** (C049, C053, C054): 0.5–1.0 / 1.9–2.3 / 0.5–1.1 / 1.8–2.0 / 0.5–0.8 %/yr; long-run mean ≈1.2–1.55%/yr.
2. **Distribution of decade TFP growth across nations** (C053, computed): mean 1.39, SD 1.49, P10 −0.33, median 1.27, P90 3.21 (n=312 country-decades, 1890s–2010s); catch-up decades reach 4–5.5%/yr.
3. **GPT arrival**: ≈1 major GPT per 50–75 years, overdispersed timing (C023, C008).
4. **GPT timing**: 1% → plateau of adoption ≈35 years (electricity); invention → peak productivity contribution ≈100 (steam), ≈40 (electricity), ≈25–30 (ICT) years; elevated contribution ≈10–20 years; preceded by ≥1 decade of below-trend measured TFP (C030, C035, C034, C038).
5. **GPT peak contribution**: ≈0.4 / 1.0 / 1.8 pp/yr (steam / electricity / ICT); sector TFP jump e.g. US manufacturing 0.5 → 5.3%/yr (C033).
6. **J-curve depth**: measured TFP level understated by up to ≈11–16% during ICT deployment (C040).
7. **International diffusion**: mean lag 42 years (median 37, P10 8, P90 82); log-lag falls ≈0.8–1.1% per year of later invention date; laggard intensity ≈47% of leader, gap widening ≈0.5%/yr for newer technologies (C045). Light-speed delay adds a hard floor (distance in light-years) but is small relative to historical lags unless interstellar distances exceed decades.
8. **AI scenario band**: +0.07 to +1.8 pp/yr for ~10 years, central ≈0.3–0.7; explosive regime only if automation share >≈50–70% (C064–C088).
9. **Model structure recommendation** (inference): add to the existing Jones/Bloom idea production function (a) a stochastic GPT arrival process, (b) a GPT "complement stock" that absorbs investment and lowers measured TFP before raising it, (c) logistic sector adoption calibrated to C032/C045, and (d) inter-nation diffusion with adoption-lag and intensity margins. Do not add an exogenous 50-year sinusoid.
