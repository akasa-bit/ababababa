## Q1. Idea production function: is research productivity falling, how fast, and what are β/φ and scale effects?

### Takeaway
Across the economy, sectors, and firms, research productivity (idea output per effective researcher) has fallen ~3–10%/yr for many decades. The aggregate US estimate is −5.1 to −5.3%/yr (half-life ~13 years) with β ≈ 3 (3.4 in BJVW Table 6). β varies widely by sector: 0.2 for Moore's law, 1–2 for firms, 2.5–7 for crops. Newer US Census microdata (2025) challenge the *firm-level* decline: TFP-on-R&D elasticities and returns roughly doubled from 1976 to 2018, while obsolescence rose. The semi-endogenous reading (growth ≈ g_S/β; population/researcher growth drives long-run growth; "Empty Planet" stagnation if population falls) is the mainstream interpretation. Time-series tests mostly reject fully endogenous (β = 0) models.

### Cited Findings
- **B001 — BJVW (2020) aggregate US research productivity.** Source: Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen & Webb, "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?", AER 110(4):1104–44 — [NBER w23782 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23782.pdf). Coverage: US, 1930s–2015. Measures: research productivity = TFP growth ÷ effective researchers (R&D spending deflated by high-skill wage). Key values: effective research effort ×23 since the 1930s (+4.3%/yr), while research productivity fell ×41 (−5.1%/yr). In Table 6 (1930–2015) the decline is −5.3%/yr, half-life 13 years, β = 3.4. In the text, "economy-wide TFP growth averages about 1.5% per year … research productivity declining … about 5% per year, yielding a β of more than 3". Research productivity fell ×8.0 from the 1960s to the 2000s and ×1.7 from the 1980s to the 2000s. Recency: 2020. Quality: the benchmark result. Its idea-output measure (TFP growth) mixes in reallocation and misallocation, and its input counts only US research. Relevance: direct calibration of β. The game's β ≈ 3.0 / −5.09%/yr / 1.67%/yr sits inside the published range (−5.1% text, −5.3% table; β 3–3.4).

### Inferences
- Semi-endogenous steady state: g_A = g_S/β. World researchers grew 3.33%/yr in 2000–2020 (own calc B103). With β = 3.0 this implies frontier TFP growth ≈ 1.1%/yr; with β = 3.4, ≈ 1.0%/yr. That is consistent with Fernald's US 1948–2025 utilization-adjusted TFP average of 1.26%/yr and post-2005 0.67%/yr (B048). The game's 1.67%/yr is closer to the 1930–2000 US experience than to the post-2005 frontier.
- β should differ by sector: semiconductors 0.2, firms ~1–2, aggregate ~3, crops 2.5–7. A per-sector β table is directly supported by B001–B005.
- Evidence that research productivity declines only "within the US frame" (BJVW count US researchers only) suggests that the relevant S for frontier growth is *global* researchers (Jones 2022). With no spillovers between nations, the game implicitly assumes each nation draws only on its own S. That contradicts B061/B063/B066 below.
- The Census-microdata challenge (B008, B009) and the stock-flow critique (B010) both point to an explicit knowledge stock with depreciation/obsolescence (δ ≈ 10–30%/yr) as a missing mechanism, rather than a different β.

### Gaps
- Direct published β/φ estimates from Jones (1995) time-series tests, Kortum (1997), Ha & Howitt (2007), and Madsen (2008) were not re-verified this session. Madsen's conclusion (often read as supporting Schumpeterian/fully endogenous models with product proliferation) is unverified.
- The exact numbers in Boeing & Hünermund come via ZEW/OECD summaries, not from the article tables.
- No reliable source found on research productivity in China or India at the aggregate (TFP-based) level beyond Boeing & Hünermund.

## Q2. R&D: intensity vs income, researchers, funding/performing shares, depreciation, lags, private/social returns, basic vs applied

### Takeaway
R&D intensity rises with income. Cross-country elasticity of R&D/GDP to GDP per capita is ~0.7 and that of researchers per million ~1.35 (own calc, Q6). The world R&D/GDP ratio rose from ~2.0% (2000) to 2.6% (2023, WDI aggregate). Business performs ~74% of OECD R&D. R&D capital depreciates 10–40%/yr (statistical agencies), with a gestation lag of ~2 years. Public/basic research reaches productivity with 8–20-year lags (up to ~30 years via interindustry spillovers). Private returns are ~10–30%. Social returns are ~2–4× private (55–58% marginal social return), and macro benefit/cost ratios are ~5–20. Government nondefense R&D returns are estimated at 150–300%. Basic research generates larger, less-internalized spillovers.

## Q3. TFP data and long-run productivity series: TFP growth by era and income level

### Takeaway
Long-run frontier TFP growth is ~1.3–1.6%/yr (1890–2024; US 1.55%/yr in the LTP/BCL data). It is hump-shaped: 1913–1973 was the golden age (US ~2%/yr; Europe and Japan 3–4%/yr catch-up in 1950–74). It slowed to ~1% in 1974–95, revived briefly to ~1.9% in the US 1995–2005, then slowed to ~0.4–0.8%/yr after 2005, and ~0.1–0.2%/yr in the post-2019 median advanced economy. The Conference Board puts global TFP growth at 0.8%/yr in 2000–07 and 0.1%/yr in 2011–19. Across countries in PWT 11.0, the median TFP growth since 1995 is ~0.4%/yr. TFP levels rise with income (ln ctfp vs ln GDP per capita slope ≈ 0.28–0.31). TFP shows conditional β-convergence since 1995.

## Q4. International technology diffusion and knowledge spillovers: adoption lags, distance/time decay, channels, convergence

### Takeaway
Most countries' productivity growth comes from foreign ideas. Eaton & Kortum find US + Japan research account for at least 2/3 of growth in each sample country. In CHH, the foreign R&D elasticity (0.21) exceeds the domestic one (0.095). Knowledge flows decay steeply with distance:
- Keller: spillovers halve at ~1,200 km.
- CDRH: interaction frequency falls 73% per 1,000 km, a half-distance of ~530 km.
- Bottazzi & Peri: regional spillovers vanish beyond ~300 km.
- Peri: 80% of knowledge stays in its origin region and 91% in its country.
- JTH: same-city citations are 5–10× the control rate, strongest for early citations.

The distance penalty falls over time. Keller's distance decay fell 20% in 1970–95, home bias in citations is falling (GLVR), and the distance effect vanishes with time since adoption (CDRH). Adoption lags average ~41–45 years historically and fell from ~50 years (pre-1950 inventions) to ~15 years (post-2000). Intensity of use in poor countries stays at about half of rich countries'. That intensity divergence, not the lags, explains most 20th-century income divergence. Absorptive capacity (R&D, human capital), trade, FDI, migrants/diasporas, and face-to-face interaction are the channels.

### Inferences
- **Distance kernels for the game.** Empirical half-distances on Earth are ~300 km (regional, B066), ~530 km (CDRH interaction frequency), and ~1,200 km (Keller, international productivity). These are frictions of human interaction, not signal delay. Light delay across Earth is only milliseconds, so measured distance decay reflects contact costs, tacit knowledge, and absorptive capacity. For the game, this implies modelling interstellar knowledge spillovers primarily as (a) a **delay** τ = light-time and (b) an **attenuation** for tacit, contact-dependent knowledge (B071, B072), not an Earth-calibrated km decay.
- **Time-decay conversion.** If knowledge loses value at an obsolescence rate δ ≈ 10–15%/yr (B028, B041), a signal delayed τ years delivers ~e^(−δτ) of its value: τ = 4.24 years (nearest star) gives 0.53–0.60. Historical adoption lags (15–45 years, B055–B057) are far longer than intra-solar-system light delays (minutes to hours). So within one star system diffusion is limited by absorption, not light speed. Between stars (years to decades), light delay becomes comparable to historical adoption lags.
- **Proposed spillover term**, consistent with CHH/EK: dA_i/dt = α S_i A_i^(1−β) + κ · h_i · Σ_j w_ij [A_j(t − τ_ij) − A_i(t)]₊, with w_ij from trade/contact. Calibration targets for κ: catch-up rates of ~2–3%/yr (Rodrik manufacturing, B074) and half-lives of ~25–120 years (B074, B085). Foreign/domestic potency is ~2/3 (EK, B063).
- **Diffusion speed has accelerated** about 3× (mean lag from 50 to 15 years). The distance penalty fell 20% in 25 years (Keller). A plausible game parameter is a slowly rising diffusion speed with communication technology, bounded by light speed.

### Gaps
- Keller (2004, JEL) "International Technology Diffusion" survey and Keller (2010, Handbook) were not fetched; their summary numbers are not cited.
- Benhabib & Spiegel (2005) logistic-diffusion estimates, Javorcik (2004) FDI backward-linkage spillovers, Agrawal & Goldfarb (2008) BITNET results, and Comin-Hobijn-Rovito usage-lag magnitudes were not verified.
- Murata et al. (2014) localization radii were not retrieved.
- No empirical source exists on knowledge diffusion under multi-year communication delays (no terrestrial analogue). Inferences above extrapolate from obsolescence rates.

## Q5. Patents as innovation measures and their link to productivity

### Takeaway
Patent counts are abundant (3.7M applications in 2024, with ~49% at China's office) but are poor cross-country measures of ideas. Resident patent applications per researcher range from 43 per 1,000 (UK, France) to 617 per 1,000 (China) (own calc). Value- and quality-weighted measures predict productivity: KPSS stock-market patent values (1-SD → +0.6–3.5% TFP over 5 years) and Kelly et al. text-based breakthrough indices (1-SD → +0.5–2 pp annual productivity growth over 5–10 years; three waves: late-19th century, 1920s–30s, post-1980). Citation counts perform worse.

### Inferences
- f(income) for researchers should be strongly convex: elasticity ~1.35, so researchers per million rise ~22× for a 10× income rise. A 10× income rise multiplies R&D intensity only ~5× (elasticity 0.71), consistent with researcher wages rising with income.
- The PWT TFP–income gradient (0.28) is much smaller than the researcher–income gradient (1.35). Without international spillovers, a nation's A should track its own cumulative research. The flat TFP gradient relative to research inputs is consistent with followers drawing on frontier knowledge (B061–B063, B079).
- Post-1990 convergence of TFP and income (B075, B108) coincides with lower adoption lags (B057). That is the empirical pattern a delayed-spillover mechanism would need to reproduce.

### Gaps
- WDI aggregates' weighting conventions (current US$ vs PPP) were not verified. The own PPP-weighted R&D/GDP (1.97% in 2021) differs from WLD (2.52%).
- No regression controls (institutions, human capital) were run. Slopes are bivariate.
- CHAT raw data were not re-analysed this session. Adoption-lag numbers come from Comin–Hobijn, Comin–Mestieri, and WIPO.
